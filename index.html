Finite generic chaining in Lean

GPT-Sol 6.2, prompted by Victor Chernozhukov

Suppose we use the largest of p standardized estimation errors to construct simultaneous confidence bounds. If all the errors equal one standard Gaussian, adding another copy leaves the maximum unchanged. If they are independent standard Gaussians, the expected maximum grows on the order of log⁡p. The two families have the same marginal distributions. What distinguishes them is the size of their increments. Generic chaining uses these increments to decide which coordinates can represent one another and how much each approximation costs.

For a finite nonempty family of vectors at and a standard Gaussian vector G, Theorem 2 identifies the geometric quantity that controls the expected maximum. With da(s,t)=‖as−at‖2, it gives

γ2(T,da)≍Emaxt∈T⟨G,at⟩.

The constants in this comparison are universal. Theorem 1 treats processes whose increments have both Gaussian and exponential tails.

1 Mixed tails and the expected maximum

Notation

Throughout, T is finite and nonempty. Write M0=1 and Mk=22k for k≥1. A sequence of nonempty nets Nk⊆T is admissible if |Nk|≤Mk. For α=1,2 define

γα(T,d)=inf(Nk) admissiblemaxt∈T∑k≥02k/αd(t,Nk).

Here d(t,Nk)=mins∈Nkd(t,s). The distance d may vanish at distinct indices. For the mixed-tail results write wk(s,t)=2kd1(s,t)+2k/2d2(s,t). For a:T→Rr and G∼N(0,Ir), write W(a)=Emaxt⟨G,at⟩ and da(s,t)=‖as−at‖2. The width uses the one-sided maximum.

Bernstein’s inequality gives two scales for an increment. The next theorem lets each scale contribute its own cost to the expected maximum.

Theorem 1. (Finite mixed-tail expectation bound)#✓

Let (Xt)t∈T be real random variables on a probability space with integrable pairwise increments. Suppose finite pseudometrics d1,d2 satisfy, for every s,t∈T and u>0,

P{|Xs−Xt|>ud2(s,t)+ud1(s,t)}≤2e−u.

Then for each t0∈T,

Emaxt|Xt−Xt0|≤48{γ2(T,d2)+γ1(T,d1)}+2maxtE|Xt−Xt0|.

Proof

The proof has two parts. Lemma 1 lets us merge nets for d1 and d2 into paths whose weighted lengths cost at most six times the sum of the two net costs. Lemma 2 bounds the expected maximum along these paths by eight times their length. Their product gives 48. At each level, group indices according to their two nearest representatives. In each group choose an index minimizing ek(t)=2kd1(t,N1k)+2k/2d2(t,N2k). The triangle inequalities give wk(t,rk(t))≤2ek(t). There are at most Mk2 groups. Take the fine nets to be all of T and terminate each path at its own index. Changing the root a to t0 costs at most E|Xa−Xt0|. Let the net costs decrease to their infima.

Uses. Lemma 1, Lemma 2, common representatives, weighted path length.

Two nets can assign different representatives to the same t. To telescope the increments along one path we must bound the cost of passing between successive representatives.

Lemma 1. (Consecutive representatives)#✓

For finite pseudometrics d1,d2, let t,p,q∈T, k≥0, and e,f∈R. If wk(t,p)≤2e and wk+1(t,q)≤2f, then

wk+1(p,q)≤4e+2f.

Proof

Apply the triangle inequality through t. The contribution of the previous representative is at most wk+1(p,t)≤2wk(p,t)≤4e. The next representative contributes at most 2f.

Uses. The two triangle inequalities and 2≤2.

Once the weighted length of every path is bounded, a single probability estimate controls the increments on all paths.

Lemma 2. (Expectation along a finite chain)#✓

Under the mixed-tail and integrability hypotheses of Theorem 1, let rk:T→T satisfy |imrk|≤Mk2 for every k≥0. Suppose r0(t)=a and rK(t)=t for all t, with K≥0. If B≥0 and ∑k=0K−1wk+1(rk(t),rk+1(t))≤B for each t, then

Emaxt|Xt−Xa|≤8B.

Proof

At each level the number of distinct links is at most the product of the numbers of representatives at its endpoints. A union bound using the mixed-tail hypothesis therefore controls every link on that level. Summing the exceptional probabilities over levels gives, for v≥1,

P{maxt|Xt−Xa|>(5+v)B}≤2e−v1−e−v.

Indeed, whenever all links satisfy their bounds, their increments telescope from a to t for every t. Integration gives the factor eight. The same estimate holds at B=0 by taking a positive upper bound for the length and letting it decrease to zero.

Uses. simultaneous tail bound, tail integration.

2 Gaussian width and comparison

For a Gaussian family, the standard deviation of an increment is its canonical distance. The next theorem determines, up to universal constants, how much this distance tells us about the expected maximum.

Theorem 2. (Finite Gaussian majorizing measures)#✓

There exist CL,CU>0 such that for every r≥0, every finite nonempty T and every a:T→Rr,

γ2(T,da)≤CLW(a),W(a)≤CUγ2(T,da).

One may take CU=100 and CL=K∗/cg, where cg and K∗ are the constants used in Lemma 5 and Lemma 6.

The two directions have separate proofs. The bound on the width uses Theorem 1. The bound on the net cost uses Lemma 5 and Lemma 6.

Proof

Bounding the width. The upper bound follows directly from Theorem 1. Gaussian increments satisfy its hypothesis with d1=0 and d2=2da. Their first absolute moments are at most da(s,t). Since the first net has only one point, diam(T,da)≤2γ2(T,da). Subtracting the centered base point preserves the expected one-sided maximum, which in turn is bounded by the expected maximum of the absolute increments. The two terms in Theorem 1 are bounded by 482γ2(T,da) and 4γ2(T,da), respectively. Taking 2≤2 gives the stated constant 100.

Constructing nets from the width. Apply Lemma 6 with F(A) equal to the Gaussian width on A for nonempty A and F(∅)=0. These values are nonnegative and monotone. Lemma 5 supplies the growth hypothesis with c=cg. We obtain γ2(T,da)≤(K∗/cg)W(a), completing the proof of Theorem 2.

Uses. Theorem 1, Lemma 5, Lemma 6.

To prove the bound on the cost of the nets, we compare the given Gaussian family with one whose maximum we can estimate.

Lemma 3. (Sudakov–Fernique comparison)#✓

Let A:T→Rr and B:T→Rs. If ‖Bi−Bj‖22≤‖Ai−Aj‖22 for all i,j, then

W(B)≤W(A).

Proof

Put the two families in orthogonal Gaussian coordinate blocks, replace the maximum by the smooth function β−1log⁡∑iexp⁡(βxi), and interpolate between the families. Gaussian integration by parts expresses the derivative of the expected smooth maximum as a sum of differences of increment variances with nonnegative weights. The hypothesis orders those variances. Integration along the interpolation and passage to the limit β→∞ give the comparison. See also van Handel, Theorem 6.8.

Uses. comparison in orthogonal coordinate blocks.

3 From local Gaussian width to a sequence of nets

Separating m small groups creates an additional contribution to their joint width. Write cg=q/4, where q is the constant in Lemma 4.

Lemma 5. (Local Gaussian growth)#✓

Let m≥1, ci∈Rr, and ai:T→Rr for 1≤i≤m. Suppose 0≤ρ≤δ/8, ‖ci−cj‖2≥δ for i≠j, and ‖ai(t)−ci‖2≤ρ for all i,t. If b∈R satisfies b≤W(ai) for every i, then

b+cgδlog⁡m≤W((ai(t))i,t).

Proof

Let H1,…,Hm be independent standard Gaussians independent of G. Compare the original process with

⟨G,ai(t)−ci⟩+(δ/4)Hi,1≤i≤m,t∈T.

Within each group the two processes have the same increments. Across different groups, the separation of the centers and the bound ρ≤δ/8 ensure that the comparison process has smaller increment variances. Lemma 3 thus gives a lower bound for the original width. To evaluate it, fix the offsets and choose the group with largest Hi. Its conditional expected maximum is at least b+(δ/4)maxiHi. Now average over the offsets and use Lemma 4.

Uses. Lemma 3, Lemma 4, increment comparison for the groups.

To turn local growth into a sequence of nets, work inside each cell A of a partition and write BA(s,h)={t∈A:d(s,t)≤h}.

Lemma 6. (Local growth controls the net cost)#✓

Let d be a finite pseudometric on T and F:2T→[0,∞) be monotone under inclusion. Suppose c>0 and, whenever h>0 and ∅≠N⊆A⊆T has pairwise distances at least h between distinct points,

F(A)≥mins∈NF(BA(s,h/8))+chlog⁡|N|.

Then γ2(T,d)≤(K∗/c)F(T), where

K∗=(2+642)/log⁡2+7682.

Proof

Within each cell, choose a remaining center whose small ball has largest F and remove a larger ball around it. Repeating this operation orders the children. A child chosen late has many earlier competitors, and the growth assumption bounds its rank in terms of the decrease in F further down the branch. Summing over intervals of three levels makes these decreases telescope. To see how the rank bound controls the number of representatives, assign a child of rank j≥1 the weight 1/(4j2) and multiply the weights along each branch. At a given threshold only a bounded number of cells can have products above the threshold. Choosing representatives from these cells produces nets with the cardinalities in the net definition. The two-point case of growth controls the cost of the first net.

Uses. greedy partition bound, cost of cutting the partition tree.

4 Application to rectangle probabilities

In the rectangle application the uniform bound on the summands gives a discrete distance. Its net cost depends only on the scale and the number of indices.

Lemma 7. (Discrete exponential-tail cost)#✓

Let |T|=m≥2, δ≥0 finite, and dδ(s,t)=δ1{s≠t}. Then

γ1(T,dδ)≤2⌊log2⁡m⌋δ.

For m=1 the cost is zero.

Proof

Use one point as long as the whole set is too large for the net. During those levels the approximation error is at most δ. Once all m points fit, the error vanishes. Summing the preceding geometric series gives the logarithmic bound. A singleton has zero approximation error at every level.

Uses. net cutoff bound.

Application to the rectangle CLT

For a centered bounded sum W with unit coordinate variances, use both signs of each coordinate and include zero as a base point. Bernstein’s inequality supplies the two increment distances in Theorem 1. The net-cost direction of Theorem 2 controls the variance distance by the Gaussian width. Lemma 7 controls the discrete envelope distance. If Z has the same covariance as W, these estimates give the rectangle paper’s bound

E‖W‖∞≤C{E‖Z‖∞+δlog⁡(ep)}.

The chaining inputs are proved in this repository. The signed-coordinate Bernstein calculation and the base-point estimate appear in Lemma C.2 of the rectangle paper.
